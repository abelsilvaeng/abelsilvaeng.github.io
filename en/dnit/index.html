Automating the Multimodal Maps

I turned DNIT's official map series into a database-driven atlas: all 27 A0 sheets now come out with no manual layout work.

The Multimodal Maps show roads, railways, waterways, ports, airfields and protected areas for each Brazilian state and for the country. Every state has its own shape and size, so each sheet used to be laid out by hand. The full set took 6 to 8 months, alongside other duties.

  • Measured the previously published maps and turned the size and position of every element into layout rules for A0 paper.
  • Organized the data in PostgreSQL/PostGIS by thematic schema and built views per paper orientation, with a mask that highlights the current state.
  • Built three atlas layouts (landscape, portrait and Brazil) where position, size and scale of each element are expressions read from the database.
  • Programmed automatic scale selection and the special insets, such as Fernando de Noronha and the Federal District.
  • Wrote technical note NT 101/2023 and the manual so the team can reproduce the process, and kept the project running through SNV release 202410A.
  • QGIS Atlas with data-defined overrides (CASE WHEN over view attributes)
  • PostGIS coverage views per paper orientation
  • Automatic scale in 1:25,000 steps
  • Layout rules derived from measuring earlier editions
  • Automated generation of all 27 maps from the database: 26 state sheets (Goiás and the Federal District share one) plus Brazil
  • 20 states in landscape and 6 in portrait, with orientation stored in the database
  • Technical note NT 101/2023 and manual signed by me

CIDE: the road network declared by the states

For 3 yearly cycles I ran the assessment and consolidation of the paved network that all 27 states report to DNIT for the CIDE fuel-tax revenue split.

Each state's paved road length is one of the criteria for sharing CIDE fuel-tax revenue (Law 10.336/2001). Every year COLEP receives a spreadsheet and, when available, vector data from each state, checks it against the federal SNV under the counting rules of Ordinance MT 197/2006 and tallies the kilometers that go on to TCU, the federal audit court.

  • Ran the assessment and consolidation for 3 cycles, from 2022-2023 to 2024-2025, working on the standard spreadsheets the office already maintained.
  • Acted as the technical contact for state road agencies: deadlines, formal channels and the data kit sent to each state.
  • Reviewed the submissions and signed state-level technical review notes.
  • Overlaid state geometry on the current SNV to flag overlapping and coincident federal segments, duplicates and segments that changed between cycles.
  • Digitized segments that came without geometry from their start and end points, checking against satellite imagery.
  • For 2024-2025, built a per-state workflow with topology checks ahead of regional and national consolidation.
  • Ordinance 197/2006 counting rules expressed as attribute filters
  • Spatial overlay between state road systems and the federal SNV
  • Cycle-over-cycle comparison by segment code
  • Per-state network topology before consolidation
  • National totals per cycle: 249,126 km (2022-2023), 253,180 km (2023-2024) and 256,101 km (2024-2025)
  • In 2024-2025: 74,009 km federal, 170,748 km state and 11,343 km of access roads longer than 0.5 km
  • 17 of the 27 states delivered georeferenced data in the 2024-2025 cycle
  • Consolidated national base with 33,503 segments from all 27 states

Lane counts for the entire road network

I assigned lane counts and shoulder classes to the federal and state road base by combining OpenStreetMap, the continuous visual survey (LVC) and the traffic volume base, to feed PNCT traffic modeling.

PNCT, the national traffic count plan, estimates annual average daily traffic through modeling, and the model needs the capacity of every segment: single or dual carriageway, how many lanes, shoulder or not. DNIT's vector base did not carry that attribute for the whole network.

  • Merged federal and state roads into one topologically consistent base with a unique key per segment.
  • Downloaded OpenStreetMap state by state, splitting the densest ones, and dropped ramps and roundabouts.
  • Built buffers per lane class and resolved overlaps with a priority rule.
  • Used SQL to pick the dominant class by length for each segment.
  • Filled what OSM did not cover with the LVC survey, the traffic volume base and classification rules.
  • Handled OSM outliers such as toll plazas and dual carriageways counted per direction.
  • Buffer-based spatial join
  • Chained symmetric difference with class priority
  • Length-weighted mode per segment
  • Imputation rules for segments with no data
  • 67.26% of the federal and state base got its lane count from OSM
  • The remaining 32.74% came from the LVC survey, the traffic volume base and rules
  • Method documented in a 2024 technical note on lane counts, signed by me

Waterway network: topology and directed routes

I cleaned the topology of the waterway base in Brazil's National Road System (SNV) and turned its pieces into continuous routes with km 0 at the river mouth.

The 2024 waterway base arrived pre-processed, split into main and secondary stretches. To become SNV routes it had to be free of dangling nodes, duplicates and multipart geometries, and run in a single direction from the mouth upstream.

  • Set up the QGIS topology checker for dangles, duplicates, invalid geometries and multiparts.
  • Found that about 92.5% of the errors sat on a single waterway and diagnosed the likely cause: the geometry had been dissolved on the wrong field.
  • Removed the hidden segments, reconnected vertices and reviewed false alarms by hand.
  • Batch-dissolved the stretches by route and fixed the multipart geometries.
  • Checked the direction of every waterway against satellite imagery and reversed the wrong ones.
  • Left a proposed method for future cases.
  • Topology rules: no dangles, no duplicates, no invalid or multipart geometry
  • Batch dissolve by route
  • Mouth-to-upstream direction checked on satellite imagery
  • 3,883 topological inconsistencies handled
  • 3,592 of them (about 92.5%) on a single waterway
  • 3 multipart geometries fixed
  • Technical note NT 0076/2024 signed by me

New geographic database and source ingestion

For DNIT's new database project I mapped the official sources and automated the ingestion of all of them, keeping origin and version traceable.

COLEP's products combine the SNV network with layers from other agencies: boundaries, hydrography, indigenous lands, quilombola territories, conservation units, ports. Without control over origin and version, a map can go out with stale data or at the wrong scale.

  • Structured the database in thematic schemas: infrastructure, layout, boundaries, places, networks and land use.
  • Mapped and loaded the external sources: IBGE (statistics and mapping), ANA (water), FUNAI, INCRA, MMA, ANTT and ANTAQ.
  • Created the metadata index with source, scale, update date, download date and usage limits for each layer.
  • Kept the database current with new SNV releases through 2025.
  • Standard ingestion: download, reprojection, validation and load by schema
  • Metadata index and version control
  • Views that hand product-ready data to the maps
  • Index with 41 registered layers: 31 from IBGE, 3 from ANA and the rest from FUNAI, INCRA, MMA, ANTT, ANTAQ and basemaps
  • The database behind the Multimodal Maps

Network for traffic volume modeling (PNCT)

I prepared the network that PNCT, the national traffic count plan, uses to estimate annual average daily traffic across the whole road system.

PNCT counts traffic at fixed stations and estimates daily volumes on the rest of the network through flow assignment. The model depends on a network with sound topology, connectors and capacity attributes. I worked on the plan's modeling by preparing that network and its modeling base.

  • Assigned lane counts and shoulder classes to the network links.
  • Organized the 2023 modeling base as a vector file and spreadsheet, with a data dictionary following the standard of DNIT's geodata portal.
  • Maintained the traffic volume series from 2018 to 2023.
  • Network with capacity attributes: surface, carriageway class, terrain and free-flow speed
  • Directional and total daily volume on every link
  • Data dictionary with the classes of each field
  • 2023 vector network with 23,181 links
  • Modeling base with 38 fields, from capacity to costs

Road naming and bridge inventory

Month after month I maintained the base of official names of federal highways, tracking bills in Congress and checking each proposal against the SNV and the laws already enacted.

Bills that name stretches of federal highways, bridges and overpasses are sent to DNIT for an opinion. COLEP checks whether the stretch exists, whether it already has a legal name and whether there is a conflict, which takes an up-to-date base of names and legislative status.

  • Updated the base every month from the open data portals of the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate, logging new bills and status changes through enactment.
  • Reviewed the bills sent to the office, with each stretch mapped onto the SNV.
  • Delivered an updated spreadsheet and a change report each month.
  • Built the base of federal bridges and other structures matched to the SNV.
  • Legislative tracking with month-by-month status history
  • Mapping stretches by highway, state and km onto the SNV
  • Conflict check against names already enacted
  • 20 monthly deliveries between June 2023 and February 2025
  • Bridge and structure base with 10,800 records located on the SNV

Federalization and transfer of road segments

I traced, release by release of the SNV, what changed in length, administration and jurisdiction on each segment, to answer requests about federalizing roads or handing federal roads over to the states.

The SNV, Brazil's National Road System, changes several times a year. When a state road becomes federal, or a federal road is passed to a state, planners need to know what changed, since when and at which km. The technical answer comes from the SNV's historical series.

  • Built a code-by-code comparison between two SNV releases, classifying each segment as unchanged, shortened or lengthened.
  • Extended the model to every SNV attribute, with a field-by-field mapping.
  • Rebuilt the history of one segment across 28 SNV releases, from 2013 to 2022, in a single analysis project.
  • Relied on the historical SNV base I had consolidated in PostgreSQL.
  • Join by segment code across releases
  • Change classification rules, later rewritten in SQL over the historical base
  • Spatial analysis with several releases overlaid
  • Comparison between releases 202101A and 202206A: 4,154 segments, 3,926 unchanged in length, 136 shorter and 92 longer
  • 104 changes of administration identified between the two releases

LVC: from survey points to lines

I turned GPS points from the continuous visual survey (LVC) into traveled stretches and productivity indicators, entirely in SQL on PostGIS.

The LVC is the visual survey of pavement surface condition on federal highways, the input to the surface condition index (ICS). The data arrived as points with date, time, chainage and direction. The office needed to see where each crew had driven and measure the output of the cycle.

  • Pre-processed the raw data, splitting date and time and standardizing columns.
  • Wrote the query that links consecutive points into lines by vehicle, highway and day, only when distance and chainage direction make sense.
  • Computed distance and time by vehicle, highway and day, plus a productivity summary by contract lot.
  • Left unproductive travel out of the averages.
  • Reused the survey to classify shoulders in the lane count project.
  • Window functions (LAG with PARTITION BY) to link consecutive points
  • 5 km gap filter and a chainage direction rule
  • PostGIS geometry: ST_MakeLine and ST_Distance
  • Indicators in km per day and km per hour
  • Survey points converted to lines at national scale
  • About 74,000 km of survey added up in one cycle, in the productivity table
  • Method documented in a 2024 technical note

Vector automation and the SNV historical database

I turned manual GIS chores (checking SNV releases, filling attributes from coincident segments, extracting geometry by km) into reusable SQL, QGIS expressions and Python scripts.

Every request at the office started the same way: find the right SNV release, cross it with another base and fill attributes by hand. I took those steps out of manual work, one at a time.

  • Created the SNV historical table in PostgreSQL, with the full data model and the release of each record.
  • Consolidated the SNV spreadsheets from 2011 to 2022 into a single load.
  • Structured the 2020 to 2022 surface condition surveys in the database, with WKT geometry.
  • Wrote a Python notebook that compares two spreadsheets by key and returns what exists in only one.
  • Wrote a QGIS expression that fills in a segment's concession operator from its coincident segments, with no manual join, plus a manual for the team.
  • Wrote a Python script that batch-extracts segment geometry by highway, state and km.
  • Versioned tables in PostgreSQL
  • Batch loads with WKT geometry
  • QGIS expressions with arrays and in-layer aggregation
  • Manuals with flowcharts so the team can reapply them
  • 30 SNV releases, 2011 to 2022, with 212,195 records in a single table
  • 2025 manual for the QGIS expression, with flowchart
